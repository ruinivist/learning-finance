# Can interest rates be negative?

## ZLB ( Zero Lower Bound )

An assumption in macro-economics that interest rates can never drop below 0%.
Argument was people will just NOT deposit money with central bank.

## ELB ( Effective Lower Bound )

But that did not hold in practice, there is a COST to holding money and even if you
ask for millions in physical cash, CB is the dictator here, and can very much charge
you for printing it. If you hold, they own the computer and can't escape interest rates.
If you don't hold, there's logistics costs to it, so much that even the penalty seems
attractive.

ELB is the somewhat negative floor for interest rates where logistics of holding cash
become more attractive than the penalty.

## Impossible Trinity ( Mundell-Fleming Trilemma )

It's one of the theorems in international economics -- the underlying basis for it is that
if money can move freely, it will just go to whoever's paying the highest interest rate.

Imagine a nation X wants to have this policy setup

- fixed exchange rate
- free capital movement
- independent monetary policy => CB controls the rates

Let's do it casewise:

1. You choose fixed rate ( 1 ) and free movement (2)
   - Denmark, Hong Kong
   - If Denmark's monetary policy were to lower rates, all the money will flee. The value
     of currency would drop in international markets ( not actually ) but as the independent
     traders stop to honor that value, government steps in ( there is participation in trading,
     just one major exchange will do since rest will be arbitraged ). If they keep on defending
     peg endlessly, they'll just run out of money.
   - If they were to raise rates, a lot of money will flee in and to honor the fixed peg, CB would
     need to keep on printing money, leading to eventual inflation.
   - So you are locked to whatever rate US / reference offers

2. You choose Fixed Rate (1) and Independent Monetary Policy (3)
   - China, Argentina
   - Then you must block capital flows to prevent the flee/overflow scenario in case 1.

3. You choose Free Movement (2) and Independent Monetary Policy (3)
   - USA, UK, Canada, Modern Switzerland
   - The CB can just give up the idea of a fixed peg entirely and call it not their responsibility.
     You do lose the reliability of a fixed peg in imports/exports and leave it to market forces ( somewhat, as CB can manage floating too )

## Money Transmission Mechanism

How does a CB printing money eventually raise prices in the shop next door?

1. When CB prints, it has to give it out ( if it doesn't it's like tree falling in a forest )
2. It can buy securities ( firms get money ), give it to banks ( cheaper credit -> firms get money ) etc, anything that goes out,
3. With more money now available, production starts to expand ( this is an expansionary policy )
4. At the current rate of production in the bakery next door, bread starts to run out ( as people have more money so they'll spend more money ) and he's forced to raise prices knowing that people will buy still.
   - or the baker will use the same cheap credit to expand production passing the ball over but that's an optimistic case and has limits too in case the country is already at full employment. The price increase is often easier to do and happens first.

The Bretton Woods system which caused almost all of developed countries to tie their value to a
fixed peg of US dollars and US in return tied it to Gold backed in Fort Knox.

This fixed peg and absolute trade certainty brought about a golden age of trade.

This collapsed when US printed too much and a "bank" run happened where countries started
exchanging their US dollars for gold and in 1971 Nixon abandoned the gold backing entirely.

This was the start of "fiat" currencies, backed only by trust in the government.

Now in 1970s too one the trusted countries was Switzerland so everyone wanted Francs and the
first NIRP started.

## Francogeddon ( Jan 15, 2015 )

### Eurozone Debt Crisis

When both the more financially capable countries like Germany and the poorer ones like Greece,
Spain, Italy all adopted euro, they got free credit boost on the back of using the same currency
and backed by same central bank ( ECB ).

This led to huge buildup of debt. In 2009, newly elected Greek government announced previous
government had been "cooking the books", a feat of Greece's debt default started and quickly
spread to other countries who were also in debt.

PIIGS : Portugal, Ireland, Italy, Greece, Spain

Normally countries with own currency can just print money to settle debts and handle the
consequences of inflation internally. But PIIGS cannot print anything here. No one was willing
to lend to these and interest rates reached unsustainable levels. The zone could've collapsed.

Except that ECB stepped in Jul 2012, then president, Mario Draghi declared that ECB will do whatever it
takes aka print money.

## Switzerland rush

Off of the crisis, now that ECB was going to be printing billions in euros and Switzerland
which was not part of EU now had to defend it's earlier decision of a fixed peg to Euro
which the ECB was set on devaluing. It could only maintain that peg by devaluing Francs at
the same rate which was senseless.

Not just ECB, there was also a rush for the safer currencies within EU so Switzerland came up
naturally.

What they ended up doing to handle both was

1. break the peg
2. deep NIRP of -0.75% to fend off investors disrupting local exporters

While the devaluation was good for Switzerland as they are more dependent on exports

### A quick note on constant devaluation

It's just "numbers" that go up, if a company in local made 100 they'll make 100000
if the currency is devalued however the inflation would make it useless, there would be
tariffs from other countries.

From the perspective of an outside buyed, it became cheaper so who eventually pays the cost?
It's from labour as think of it this way. You can't cut wages in half to make it cheaper
for a buyer but you just have CB devalue it so it now means half of it but numerically is the
same. It's the wages that get reduced. The global purchasing power of the citizens is the
tradeoff.

### Switzerland's devaluation stop

Note that Switzerland was independent but to maintain peg to euro it had to hold huge amounts
of euro, all the while ECB was printing more and more euro. Keeping a fixed peg to a currency
that's losing confidence and the constant devaluation of Francs had to be stopped.
